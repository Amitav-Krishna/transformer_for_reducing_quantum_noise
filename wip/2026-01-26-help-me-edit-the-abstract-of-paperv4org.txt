  specific concept in quantum mechanics. Your title says "Frobenius
  Normalization"—that's the accurate term for what you're doing (normalizing by
  ||ρ||_F).

   whether the abstract needs to be self-contained on that detail.

❯ Wait, I'm dumb, what does a frobenius norm actually measure? 

● It's the "size" of a matrix—treat the matrix as a vector of all its entries
  and take the Euclidean length.

  ||A||_F = sqrt(Σ|a_ij|²) = sqrt(Tr(A†A))

  For a density matrix ρ (which is Hermitian), this simplifies to
  sqrt(Tr(ρ²))—which is sqrt(purity).

  So:
  - Pure state: purity = 1, ||ρ||_F = 1
  - Maximally mixed (ρ = I/d): purity = 1/d, ||ρ||_F = 1/sqrt(d)

  Noisy states get pushed toward maximally mixed, so their Frobenius norm
  shrinks. That's the scale mismatch your normalization fixes—noisy inputs are
  "smaller" than clean targets.

❯ So is purity normalization accurate or not? 

● It's technically accurate—Frobenius normalizing a density matrix scales it to
  have Tr(ρ²) = 1, i.e., purity = 1.

  But it could mislead: the result isn't a pure state (which also requires ρ² =
  ρ and Tr(ρ) = 1). You're just scaling to unit purity, not projecting onto a
  pure state.

  "Frobenius normalization" describes the operation. "Purity normalization"
  describes the outcome but might make readers think you're doing something more
   physical. I'd stick with Frobenius.

❯ Why is the scale mismatch between the noisy and noiseless states worse at 
  larger circuit sizes?                                                     

● The maximally mixed state (where noise pushes you) is I/2^n. Its Frobenius
  norm is:

  ||I/2^n||_F = sqrt(Tr(I/2^(2n))) = sqrt(2^n / 2^(2n)) = 1/sqrt(2^n)

  So:
  - 5 qubits: noisy floor ≈ 1/sqrt(32) ≈ 0.18
  - 8 qubits: noisy floor = 1/sqrt(256) = 0.0625

  Clean states stay near ||ρ||_F ≈ 1 (pure states have norm exactly 1). The gap
  grows exponentially with qubit count—at 8 qubits the model needs to rescale by
   ~16× instead of ~6×. Without normalization, that scale recovery dominates the
   learning problem.

❯ Explain simply what types of tasks quantum computers can actually         
  speedup, like as in what traits those tasks have                          

● Quantum speedups generally come from two things:

  1. Problems with hidden structure (like periodicity or symmetry): Quantum
  algorithms can use interference to find patterns that classical algorithms
  have to search for exhaustively. Shor's algorithm exploits the periodic
  structure hidden in modular arithmetic to factor numbers.
  2. Simulating quantum systems: Quantum computers are naturally good at
  simulating other quantum things (molecules, materials) because they are
  quantum. Classically, simulating n qubits requires tracking 2^n amplitudes. A
  quantum computer just... is that.

  The common thread: you need a problem where you can set up superposition over
  many possibilities, then use interference to amplify correct answers and
  cancel wrong ones. If a problem doesn't have structure that interference can
  exploit, you don't get much speedup.

  Quantum computers are not a general "do everything faster" upgrade—they're
  more like specialized hardware for problems with the right mathematical shape.

❯ Explain simply why interference makes problems with hidden structure are  
  particularly well-suited to quantum computers                             

● When a quantum computer explores possibilities in superposition, each path
  carries a phase (a complex number). At the end, paths combine—same phase adds
  up (constructive), opposite phase cancels (destructive).

  If a problem has structure, that structure creates correlations between paths.
   Paths leading to the right answer end up in phase with each other; paths
  leading to wrong answers end up out of phase and cancel.

  Without structure, the phases are essentially random—interference is just
  noise, nothing systematic cancels or reinforces, and you're no better off than
   classical guessing.

  So it's not that quantum computers "search faster." It's that structure lets
  you choreograph the interference so probability pools at the answer. No
  structure, no choreography, no speedup.

❯ Is each path a qubit, or can multiple paths be contained in a single      
  qubit?                                                                    

● Multiple paths come from multiple qubits together. With n qubits, you get 2^n
  basis states (like |00101...⟩), and the system can be in superposition over
  all of them. Each basis state is a "path."

  A single qubit only gives you 2 paths (|0⟩ and |1⟩). The exponential comes
  from combining qubits—each additional qubit doubles the number of paths.
  That's both the power (exponentially many paths) and the limitation (you need
  n qubits to explore 2^n possibilities).

❯ The noise in this case would be correlations with the bath? 

● That's the big one, yes—decoherence. The qubits become entangled with the
  environment (the bath), and that entanglement effectively "leaks" phase
  information out of your system. Once the environment has partial information
  about your qubit states, the interference patterns degrade because the phases
  are no longer well-defined within just your system.

  There's also:
  - Gate errors: rotations aren't quite right
  - Readout errors: detector misreads 0/1
  - Crosstalk: operating on one qubit disturbs neighbors

  But decoherence (bath correlations) is the fundamental one—it's why quantum
  computation is a race against time. The interference you're trying to
  choreograph falls apart as the system entangles with everything around it.

❯ So all quantum computers will eventually leak all of the information to   
  the environmnet? Is this what is meant by maximally mixed?                

● Exactly right. If you let a quantum computer sit long enough (or run through
  enough noisy operations), the qubits become fully entangled with the
  environment. From your perspective—only looking at the qubits, not the
  environment—all the information is gone. You're left with maximum uncertainty
  about the state.

  That's the maximally mixed state: ρ = I/d. It says "each basis state is
  equally likely, I know nothing." It's the quantum equivalent of a uniform
  distribution over all possibilities.

  The information isn't destroyed—it's still there in the joint
  system+environment state. But it's inaccessible to you. From your local view,
  it's been erased. That's why decoherence is so damaging: your carefully
  choreographed interference pattern gets smeared into "could be anything."

❯ Wait, why can't both me and the environment know about this? What if I    
  become the environment?                                                   

● This is the key weirdness of quantum information: it can't be copied
  (no-cloning theorem). If the environment becomes correlated with your qubit,
  that correlation is the information transfer—it's not that a copy was made,
  it's that the information now lives in the correlations between system and
  environment.

  If you could "become the environment"—meaning capture every degree of freedom
  your qubits interacted with (every air molecule, stray photon, phonon in the
  substrate)—you could in principle do a joint measurement on system+environment
   and recover the full pure state. The information isn't destroyed, it's
  delocalized.

  But practically, "the environment" means 10^23 uncontrolled particles. The
  information scrambles into correlations you can't access. That's why
  decoherence looks irreversible even though unitary quantum mechanics is
  reversible in principle.

  So it's not that you can't know—it's that knowing requires controlling
  everything your system touched. The universe still has the information; you
  just don't.

❯ In the case of molecule simulation, the environment would have full       
  information about what the molecule looks like but not me?                

● Not quite—the environment doesn't "know" anything in a useful sense. The
  information isn't sitting somewhere as "here's the molecule's structure." It's
   smeared across correlations between your qubits and billions of air
  molecules, photons, vibrations in the chip, etc.

  It's less "the environment has the answer" and more "the answer got shredded
  into correlations that nobody can piece back together." The environment isn't
  an observer that learned something—it's just stuff that became entangled with
  your computation.

  So after decoherence: you don't have the answer, the environment doesn't
  "have" the answer in any usable sense, and reconstructing it would require
  tracking every particle your qubits touched. The information exists in
  principle but is practically gone.

  That's why QSR matters—you're trying to recover what you can from the partial
  information that's still in your system before it all leaks out.

❯ OK, I'll make that correction but explain just to me in more detail 

● In a typical quantum algorithm:

  1. Spread out: You apply gates (like Hadamard) that put qubits into
  superposition, creating many computational paths in parallel
  2. Compute: You apply operations that act on all paths simultaneously. Each
  path accumulates a phase (a complex number) based on what happens along that
  path
  3. Bring together: You apply more gates (often another Hadamard, or
  problem-specific operations) that cause paths to combine. When paths combine:
    - Same phase → amplitudes add (constructive interference) → higher
  probability
    - Opposite phase → amplitudes cancel (destructive interference) → lower
  probability

  The "bringing together" is literally applying a gate that mixes basis states.
  A Hadamard on |0⟩ and |1⟩ doesn't just flip a coin—it creates a superposition
  where both states contribute to the output amplitude. If you had |0⟩ with
  phase +1 and |1⟩ with phase -1, a Hadamard combines them and they cancel in
  one output, reinforce in another.

  Shor's algorithm is the classic example: the quantum Fourier transform at the
  end is exactly this "bring together" step—it's designed so that paths
  corresponding to the correct period interfere constructively, while wrong
  answers cancel.

❯ How does the quantum computer "know" the correct answer though? 

● It doesn't "know"—the algorithm is designed so the math works out.

  Take Shor's algorithm for factoring:

  1. You want to find the period r of f(x) = aˣ mod N
  2. You create superposition over all x values and compute f(x) for each
  3. Key insight: all x values that differ by r give the same f(x). So they're
  linked—they form a periodic pattern in your superposition
  4. The Quantum Fourier Transform is a specific set of gates that, by its
  mathematical definition, converts periodic signals into frequency peaks. Apply
   it to your periodic superposition, and it produces peaks at multiples of 1/r
  5. Why do the right frequencies get amplified? Because inputs spaced r apart
  all contribute with phases that align at frequency 1/r. At other frequencies,
  the phases point in random directions around the unit circle and cancel.

  The "knowledge" is in three places:
  - The problem structure (periodicity exists)
  - The algorithm design (QFT converts periodicity to peaks)
  - The physics (interference is what it is)

  The quantum computer is just following physics. The cleverness is in the
  algorithm designer figuring out how to encode the problem so that correct
  answers happen to constructively interfere. It's like asking how a lens
  "knows" where to focus—it doesn't, the geometry just works out that way.

   state and destroy your computation. So how do you detect errors without

  - The actual information lives in the correlations between qubits
  - Errors create detectable patterns that don't reveal the information itself

  - |1⟩ → |111⟩

  2. "Are qubits 2 and 3 the same?" → Yes

  asked "what is the value?"—just "are they matching?" The parity check reveals
  where the error is without revealing what the logical state is.

  You entangle them with your data qubits briefly, transferring only the "is
  there an error?" information, then measure the ancillas. The data qubits never

❯ What does applying the correction actually look like?

  3. Classical computer tells quantum hardware: "apply X to qubit 1"
  4. Quantum hardware applies the X gate

  classical computer (just a lookup table or, in fancier setups, a neural

  qubit.

  that don't require extra qubits, just classical post-processing.

❯ How expensive is a physical qubit?  

● Hard to pin down exactly, but ballpark:

  Superconducting qubits (IBM, Google):
  - Need dilution refrigerators (~$1-2M) that cool to 10-20 millikelvin
  - Control electronics, wiring, calibration
  - Rough estimates: $10K–$100K+ per qubit when you factor in infrastructure

  Trapped ions (IonQ, Quantinuum):
  - Ultra-high vacuum systems, precision lasers
  - Different cost structure, still expensive

  The per-qubit cost is hard to isolate because so much is shared
  infrastructure. But the point stands: if you need 1,000 physical qubits per
  logical qubit, and you want 1,000 logical qubits for a useful computation,
  you're talking about a million physical qubits. Even at $10K each that's $10
  billion in hardware, plus a warehouse of dilution refrigerators.

  That's why "near-term quantum computing" is its own field—we need ways to do
  useful things with the ~100–1,000 noisy qubits we can actually build today.

❯ QEM is purely classical, right? 

● Not quite—it's a hybrid:

  1. Quantum: You still run the circuit on the quantum computer. Often you run
  it multiple times with variations (different noise levels, extra gates
  inserted, etc.)
  2. Classical: You combine the measurement outcomes from those runs to estimate
   what the noise-free result would have been

  Common QEM techniques:
  - Zero-noise extrapolation: Run at artificially increased noise levels,
  extrapolate back to zero
  - Probabilistic error cancellation: Insert random operations that
  statistically cancel out noise effects
  - Measurement error mitigation: Calibrate your detectors, correct for readout
  mistakes

  So it's not "purely classical"—you need the quantum hardware. But the
  correction happens in classical post-processing, not by adding physical
  qubits. That's the key difference from QEC.

  The catch: QEM typically increases the number of circuit runs you need
  (sampling overhead), and it only recovers expectation values—not the full
  quantum state. That's where your QSR work comes in.

❯ How does one artificially increase noise? 

● Two main tricks:

  Gate folding: Instead of applying gate G, apply G G† G (gate, inverse, gate).
  Mathematically it's identical to just G, but you've tripled the operations, so
   you've tripled the noise exposure. Repeat to get higher noise levels: G (G†
  G)^n.

  Pulse stretching: Each gate is physically implemented by a control pulse
  (microwave for superconducting, laser for ions). Stretch the pulse duration →
  qubit sits there longer → more time to decohere.

  So you're not injecting fake noise—you're increasing exposure to the real
  noise that's already there. Run at 1x, 1.5x, 2x noise, measure the results,
  fit a curve, extrapolate back to 0x.

  The assumption is that the noise scales predictably. If it does, extrapolation
   works. If the noise has weird nonlinear behavior at low levels, your
  extrapolation might be off.

❯ Wait, QSR is also a form of correction that is purely classical, correct? 
  Would it be appropriate to say that QSR is similar to QEM just operating  
  on a different attribute of the quantum system.                           

● That's a reasonable framing. Both are:
  - Classical post-processing to correct for quantum noise
  - No extra qubits required
  - Applied after you've run the quantum circuit

  The difference is what you're recovering:
  - QEM → expectation values (single numbers like ⟨Z⟩)
  - QSR → full density matrix (the complete quantum state)

  And consequently, what measurements you need:
  - QEM → can work with computational basis measurements
  - QSR → requires tomography (measurements in many bases)

  So yes, you could frame QSR as "QEM's cousin that operates on the full state
  instead of expectation values." They're both in the family of "classical
  post-processing to mitigate quantum noise," just targeting different outputs.

  That might actually be a nice way to position it in your intro—readers
  familiar with QEM will immediately get what QSR is about.

❯ What are some approaches to QSR apart from ML? 

● Several traditional approaches:

  Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE): Find the density matrix that maximizes
  the probability of your observed measurements. Iterative optimization,
  guarantees a valid density matrix. The workhorse of QSR.

  Linear Inversion: Directly invert the tomographic equations. Fast, but often
  produces invalid matrices (negative eigenvalues). Needs post-hoc fixing.

  Convex optimization / Semidefinite programming: Formulate QSR as convex
  optimization with constraints (positive semidefinite, trace 1). Guaranteed
  global optimum, but computationally heavy.

  Bayesian methods: Put a prior over density matrices, update with data, get a
  posterior distribution. Gives uncertainty estimates, not just point estimates.

  Compressed sensing: Assumes the state is low-rank (limited entanglement). Can
  reconstruct with fewer measurements than full tomography. Good for pure or
  nearly-pure states.

  Matrix Product State tomography: For large systems, assumes limited
  entanglement structure. Scales much better than full tomography but only works
   for states with that structure.

  The ML advantage: traditional methods solve each reconstruction independently,
   while ML can learn patterns across many states and (ideally) generalize. The
  tradeoff is you need training data and the model might not generalize to
  out-of-distribution noise.

❯ Wouldn't MLE be tomogrophy, not QSR? 

● You're raising a good terminological point. Let me clarify:

  QST (Quantum State Tomography): measurements → density matrix estimate. MLE,
  linear inversion, Bayesian methods all live here. They take raw measurement
  data and produce a state.

  QSR (Quantum State Reconstruction): In your paper's framing, this is noisy
  density matrix → clean density matrix. You assume tomography already happened;
   you're denoising the result.

  So MLE is tomography—it's the step before what your paper does. Your ML models
   take the output of tomography (a noisy ρ) and clean it up.

  The terminology is a bit muddled in the literature—some papers use
  "reconstruction" and "tomography" interchangeably. But in your framing, the
  distinction is:

  1. Run circuit on noisy hardware
  2. Do tomography (MLE, etc.) → get noisy ρ
  3. Do reconstruction (your ML) → get cleaner ρ

  So you're right to distinguish them. Your related work section might want to
  clarify this: MLE etc. are tomography methods, not competitors to your
  denoising approach—they're the upstream step that produces your input.

❯ Wait, I'm dumb, what does a frobenius norm actually measure? 

● It's the "size" of a matrix—treat the matrix as a vector of all its entries
  and take the Euclidean length.

  ||A||_F = sqrt(Σ|a_ij|²) = sqrt(Tr(A†A))

  For a density matrix ρ (which is Hermitian), this simplifies to
  sqrt(Tr(ρ²))—which is sqrt(purity).

  So:
  - Pure state: purity = 1, ||ρ||_F = 1
  - Maximally mixed (ρ = I/d): purity = 1/d, ||ρ||_F = 1/sqrt(d)

  Noisy states get pushed toward maximally mixed, so their Frobenius norm
  shrinks. That's the scale mismatch your normalization fixes—noisy inputs are
  "smaller" than clean targets.

❯ So is purity normalization accurate or not? 

● It's technically accurate—Frobenius normalizing a density matrix scales it to
  have Tr(ρ²) = 1, i.e., purity = 1.

  But it could mislead: the result isn't a pure state (which also requires ρ² =
  ρ and Tr(ρ) = 1). You're just scaling to unit purity, not projecting onto a
  pure state.

  "Frobenius normalization" describes the operation. "Purity normalization"
  describes the outcome but might make readers think you're doing something more
   physical. I'd stick with Frobenius.

❯ Why is the scale mismatch between the noisy and noiseless states worse at 
  larger circuit sizes?                                                     

● The maximally mixed state (where noise pushes you) is I/2^n. Its Frobenius
  norm is:

  ||I/2^n||_F = sqrt(Tr(I/2^(2n))) = sqrt(2^n / 2^(2n)) = 1/sqrt(2^n)

  So:
  - 5 qubits: noisy floor ≈ 1/sqrt(32) ≈ 0.18
  - 8 qubits: noisy floor = 1/sqrt(256) = 0.0625

  Clean states stay near ||ρ||_F ≈ 1 (pure states have norm exactly 1). The gap
  grows exponentially with qubit count—at 8 qubits the model needs to rescale by
   ~16× instead of ~6×. Without normalization, that scale recovery dominates the
   learning problem.

❯ Explain simply what types of tasks quantum computers can actually         
  speedup, like as in what traits those tasks have                          

● Quantum speedups generally come from two things:

  1. Problems with hidden structure (like periodicity or symmetry): Quantum
  algorithms can use interference to find patterns that classical algorithms
  have to search for exhaustively. Shor's algorithm exploits the periodic
  structure hidden in modular arithmetic to factor numbers.
  2. Simulating quantum systems: Quantum computers are naturally good at
  simulating other quantum things (molecules, materials) because they are
  quantum. Classically, simulating n qubits requires tracking 2^n amplitudes. A
  quantum computer just... is that.

  The common thread: you need a problem where you can set up superposition over
  many possibilities, then use interference to amplify correct answers and
  cancel wrong ones. If a problem doesn't have structure that interference can
  exploit, you don't get much speedup.

  Quantum computers are not a general "do everything faster" upgrade—they're
  more like specialized hardware for problems with the right mathematical shape.

❯ Explain simply why interference makes problems with hidden structure are  
  particularly well-suited to quantum computers                             

● When a quantum computer explores possibilities in superposition, each path
  carries a phase (a complex number). At the end, paths combine—same phase adds
  up (constructive), opposite phase cancels (destructive).

  If a problem has structure, that structure creates correlations between paths.
   Paths leading to the right answer end up in phase with each other; paths
  leading to wrong answers end up out of phase and cancel.

  Without structure, the phases are essentially random—interference is just
  noise, nothing systematic cancels or reinforces, and you're no better off than
   classical guessing.

  So it's not that quantum computers "search faster." It's that structure lets
  you choreograph the interference so probability pools at the answer. No
  structure, no choreography, no speedup.

❯ Is each path a qubit, or can multiple paths be contained in a single      
  qubit?                                                                    

● Multiple paths come from multiple qubits together. With n qubits, you get 2^n
  basis states (like |00101...⟩), and the system can be in superposition over
  all of them. Each basis state is a "path."

  A single qubit only gives you 2 paths (|0⟩ and |1⟩). The exponential comes
  from combining qubits—each additional qubit doubles the number of paths.
  That's both the power (exponentially many paths) and the limitation (you need
  n qubits to explore 2^n possibilities).

❯ The noise in this case would be correlations with the bath? 

● That's the big one, yes—decoherence. The qubits become entangled with the
  environment (the bath), and that entanglement effectively "leaks" phase
  information out of your system. Once the environment has partial information
  about your qubit states, the interference patterns degrade because the phases

  it's been erased. That's why decoherence is so damaging: your carefully

  it's that the information now lives in the correlations between system and
  environment.

  If you could "become the environment"—meaning capture every degree of freedom
  your qubits interacted with (every air molecule, stray photon, phonon in the

  delocalized.

  information scrambles into correlations you can't access. That's why
  decoherence looks irreversible even though unitary quantum mechanics is
  reversible in principle.

  So it's not that you can't know—it's that knowing requires controlling
  everything your system touched. The universe still has the information; you
  just don't.

❯ OK, I'll make that correction but explain just to me in more detail 

● In a typical quantum algorithm:

  1. Spread out: You apply gates (like Hadamard) that put qubits into
  superposition, creating many computational paths in parallel
  2. Compute: You apply operations that act on all paths simultaneously. Each
  path accumulates a phase (a complex number) based on what happens along that
  path
  3. Bring together: You apply more gates (often another Hadamard, or
  problem-specific operations) that cause paths to combine. When paths combine:
    - Same phase → amplitudes add (constructive interference) → higher
  probability
    - Opposite phase → amplitudes cancel (destructive interference) → lower
  probability

  The "bringing together" is literally applying a gate that mixes basis states.
  A Hadamard on |0⟩ and |1⟩ doesn't just flip a coin—it creates a superposition
  where both states contribute to the output amplitude. If you had |0⟩ with
  phase +1 and |1⟩ with phase -1, a Hadamard combines them and they cancel in
  one output, reinforce in another.

  Shor's algorithm is the classic example: the quantum Fourier transform at the
  end is exactly this "bring together" step—it's designed so that paths
  corresponding to the correct period interfere constructively, while wrong
  answers cancel.

❯ How does the quantum computer "know" the correct answer though? 

● It doesn't "know"—the algorithm is designed so the math works out.

  Take Shor's algorithm for factoring:

  1. You want to find the period r of f(x) = aˣ mod N
  2. You create superposition over all x values and compute f(x) for each
  3. Key insight: all x values that differ by r give the same f(x). So they're
  linked—they form a periodic pattern in your superposition
  4. The Quantum Fourier Transform is a specific set of gates that, by its
  mathematical definition, converts periodic signals into frequency peaks. Apply
   it to your periodic superposition, and it produces peaks at multiples of 1/r
  5. Why do the right frequencies get amplified? Because inputs spaced r apart
  all contribute with phases that align at frequency 1/r. At other frequencies,
  the phases point in random directions around the unit circle and cancel.

  The "knowledge" is in three places:
  - The problem structure (periodicity exists)
  - The algorithm design (QFT converts periodicity to peaks)
  - The physics (interference is what it is)

  The quantum computer is just following physics. The cleverness is in the
  algorithm designer figuring out how to encode the problem so that correct
  answers happen to constructively interfere. It's like asking how a lens
  "knows" where to focus—it doesn't, the geometry just works out that way.

  - Errors create detectable patterns that don't reveal the information itself

  - |1⟩ → |111⟩

  2. "Are qubits 2 and 3 the same?" → Yes

  asked "what is the value?"—just "are they matching?" The parity check reveals
  where the error is without revealing what the logical state is.

  You entangle them with your data qubits briefly, transferring only the "is

❯ QEM is purely classical, right? 

● Not quite—it's a hybrid:

  1. Quantum: You still run the circuit on the quantum computer. Often you run
  it multiple times with variations (different noise levels, extra gates
  inserted, etc.)
  2. Classical: You combine the measurement outcomes from those runs to estimate
   what the noise-free result would have been

  Common QEM techniques:
  - Zero-noise extrapolation: Run at artificially increased noise levels,
  extrapolate back to zero
  - Probabilistic error cancellation: Insert random operations that
  statistically cancel out noise effects
  - Measurement error mitigation: Calibrate your detectors, correct for readout
  mistakes

  So it's not "purely classical"—you need the quantum hardware. But the
  correction happens in classical post-processing, not by adding physical
  qubits. That's the key difference from QEC.

  The catch: QEM typically increases the number of circuit runs you need
  (sampling overhead), and it only recovers expectation values—not the full
  quantum state. That's where your QSR work comes in.

❯ How does one artificially increase noise? 

● Two main tricks:

  Gate folding: Instead of applying gate G, apply G G† G (gate, inverse, gate).
  Mathematically it's identical to just G, but you've tripled the operations, so
   you've tripled the noise exposure. Repeat to get higher noise levels: G (G†
  G)^n.

  Pulse stretching: Each gate is physically implemented by a control pulse
  (microwave for superconducting, laser for ions). Stretch the pulse duration →
  qubit sits there longer → more time to decohere.

  So you're not injecting fake noise—you're increasing exposure to the real
  noise that's already there. Run at 1x, 1.5x, 2x noise, measure the results,
  fit a curve, extrapolate back to 0x.

  The assumption is that the noise scales predictably. If it does, extrapolation
   works. If the noise has weird nonlinear behavior at low levels, your
  extrapolation might be off.

❯ What are some approaches to QSR apart from ML? 

● Several traditional approaches:

  Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE): Find the density matrix that maximizes
  the probability of your observed measurements. Iterative optimization,
  guarantees a valid density matrix. The workhorse of QSR.

  Linear Inversion: Directly invert the tomographic equations. Fast, but often
  produces invalid matrices (negative eigenvalues). Needs post-hoc fixing.

  Convex optimization / Semidefinite programming: Formulate QSR as convex
  optimization with constraints (positive semidefinite, trace 1). Guaranteed
  global optimum, but computationally heavy.

  Bayesian methods: Put a prior over density matrices, update with data, get a
  posterior distribution. Gives uncertainty estimates, not just point estimates.

  Compressed sensing: Assumes the state is low-rank (limited entanglement). Can
  reconstruct with fewer measurements than full tomography. Good for pure or
  nearly-pure states.

  Matrix Product State tomography: For large systems, assumes limited
  entanglement structure. Scales much better than full tomography but only works
   for states with that structure.

  The ML advantage: traditional methods solve each reconstruction independently,
   while ML can learn patterns across many states and (ideally) generalize. The
  tradeoff is you need training data and the model might not generalize to
  out-of-distribution noise.

❯ Wouldn't MLE be tomogrophy, not QSR? 

● You're raising a good terminological point. Let me clarify:

  QST (Quantum State Tomography): measurements → density matrix estimate. MLE,
  linear inversion, Bayesian methods all live here. They take raw measurement
  data and produce a state.

  QSR (Quantum State Reconstruction): In your paper's framing, this is noisy
  density matrix → clean density matrix. You assume tomography already happened;
   you're denoising the result.

  So MLE is tomography—it's the step before what your paper does. Your ML models
   take the output of tomography (a noisy ρ) and clean it up.

  The terminology is a bit muddled in the literature—some papers use
  "reconstruction" and "tomography" interchangeably. But in your framing, the
  distinction is:

  1. Run circuit on noisy hardware
  2. Do tomography (MLE, etc.) → get noisy ρ
  3. Do reconstruction (your ML) → get cleaner ρ

  So you're right to distinguish them. Your related work section might want to
  clarify this: MLE etc. are tomography methods, not competitors to your
  denoising approach—they're the upstream step that produces your input.
